Ptosis Management

Ptosis Overview & Types

Ptosis is the drooping of the upper eyelid. It can be congenital, aponeurotic or involutional, myogenic (due to myasthenia gravis, CPEO), neurogenic (due to third nerve palsy), or mechanical (due to any lesion on the upper lid).

Symptoms & Clinical Signs (astigmatism, amblyopia)

Drooping of the upper lid blocking vision, chin up position, astigmatism, amblyopia (especially in congenital ptosis), and constant use of eyebrows to lift the upper lid leading to eye strain, heaviness, and tiredness.

Surgical & Medical Treatment

Almost always surgical. Congenital ptosis utilizes frontalis flap/sling surgery, maximal levator resection, or fascia lata sling. Aponeurotic uses levator advancement or resection. Myogenic/neurogenic use crutch glasses and targeted surgeries, while mechanical ptosis focuses on treating the underlying cause.

Ectropion & Entropion Management

Ectropion (Outward Turning of Eyelid)

Most commonly occurs due to aging, but can also happen due to trauma and previous surgery. Symptoms include redness, dryness, and epiphora (watering). Treatment is mainly surgical.

Entropion (Inward Turning of Eyelid)

Most commonly happens due to aging, trauma, previous surgery, or conditions like Stevens-Johnson syndrome. The eyelashes touch the ocular surface causing severe irritation, redness, and epiphora. Treatment is primarily surgical, though Botox can be used temporarily for patients unfit for surgery.

Orbit and Socket Related Problems

1. Proptosis Management

Specialized surgeries including anterior orbitotomy and lateral orbitotomy for accessing and removing orbital lesions causing prominent eyes.

2. Thyroid Eye Disease

Thyroid eye disease is an autoimmune disease causing inflammation and swelling of structures around the eye. It can cause swelling of extraocular muscles and orbital fat leading to optic nerve compression. It features an active inflammatory phase (treated with intravenous steroids, radiation, or second-line drugs like rituximab/tocilizumab) and an inactive stage (managed with rehabilitative surgeries like orbital decompression, squint surgery, and lid retraction repair).

3. Orbital Fracture Repairs & Cellulitis

Paediatric and adult orbital fracture repairs using titanium implants/osteomesh, alongside aggressive management for orbital cellulitis.

4. Socket Reconstruction

Socket reconstruction rebuilds anophthalmic sockets using orbital implants, mucous membrane grafts, and dermis fat grafts. Common surgeries include evisceration/enucleation with ball implants (PMMA). Dermis fat grafts are harvested from the abdomen/buttocks; mucous membrane grafts from the lips/buccal cavity deepen shallow fornices. Artificial eye placement occurs after ~6 weeks for evisceration/enucleation or ~2 months for grafts.

5. Inflammatory & Traumatic Optic Conditions

Management of lacrimal gland swellings, lymphoma, optic neuritis, myositis, and traumatic optic nerve damage.

Epiphora (Watering From Eyes)

Epiphora Overview & Definition

Epiphora is the medical terminology for excessive watering from the eyes.

Causes of Epiphora

1. Blockage in the tear drainage pathway: Narrow or a blocked punctum, upper or lower canalicular stenosis/blockage, common canalicular blockage, canaliculitis, and nasolacrimal duct blockage.

2. Eyelid abnormalities: Lid laxity, ectropion, entropion, epiblepharon, euryblepharon, and trichiasis (congenital or acquired).

3. Ocular & Surface Abnormalities: Allergy, infection, inflammation, corneal/surface foreign body, pterygium, and conjunctivochalasis (loose conjunctiva).

4. Dry Eye & MGD: Environmental dryness or secondary to Sjogren's syndrome, post-LASIK causing reflex watering, plus meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD).

5. Excessive Production: Hypersecretion of tears can also lead to constant watering.

Treatment Options

Tear Drainage Blockage (Surgical): External or Endoscopic DCR with stenting for nasolacrimal duct blockage; trephination with/without DCR and stenting for canalicular blockage; 3-snip procedure for punctal stenosis; and medical or canaliculotomy with curettage for canaliculitis.

Other Management: Surgical correction for lid abnormalities and conjunctivochalasis; medical management, hot fomentation, and IPL therapy for MGD; tear substitutes for dry eye; and medical protocols for infections/allergies.

Oculofacial Trauma

1. Overview & Causes

Oculofacial trauma involves physical injury to the eyelid and adnexal structures, eye, face, and the bony orbit surrounding the eye.

Most common causes: Assault, road traffic accidents, self-falls, and trauma with a blunt or sharp object.

2. Signs & Symptoms

Clinical presentation includes redness of the eye, subconjunctival haemorrhage, swelling around the eye, extraocular movement restriction, diminution or loss of vision, and double vision.

Also includes lid and oculofacial lacerations with or without skin loss, restriction of jaw movements, and compartment syndrome (tightness around the eye or a frozen globe).

3. Diagnosis & Patient Stabilization

Requires a thorough ocular examination to rule out any intraocular or optic nerve injury, along with a CT scan of the face and head to check for facial fractures, muscle entrapment, and to rule out head injury. Patient stabilization is the primary initial step.

4. Surgical & Medical Management

Paediatric Fractures: Also called white-eyed fractures, requiring immediate surgery to release entrapped muscle and restore normal eye movement.

Adult Orbital Floor Fractures: Causing double vision, muscle entrapment, and severe enophthalmos require surgical intervention with an implant (best results within the first 2 weeks).

ZMC & Comminuted Fractures: Require open reduction and internal fixation with screws, plates, and implants using a multidisciplinary approach (Oculoplasty and Maxillofacial surgeons).

Lacerations & Neuropathy: Lid, canalicular, and oculofacial lacerations require reconstructive surgery; traumatic optic neuropathy requires immediate high-dose intravenous steroids.

Ocular Surface Disorders & Tumors
